Section B — Statement 9Films, as a medium, have always championed artistic expression and individuality since their inception; two directors who still adhere to this are Wes Anderson and Hayao Miyazaki. However, films have a long [history of being] part of a modern industry inherently linked to commercialisation. While this places limits on an individual's ability to experiment with films, creativity also requires limitations, and therefore the commercialisation of cinema has not only always been inevitable (not a 21st-century phenomenon), but is far from 'barbaric' as the statement suggests.   Wes Anderson's The Grand Budapest Hotel [uses a] stylised filmmaking technique [unique] to Anderson — [one] that laments the [loss of] the past, [to] express the beauty of the "world of yesterday". And in this way, Anderson seemingly adheres to the contention that cinema [is] suffering from [commercialised] barbarity. As an artist then, he uses The Grand Budapest Hotel's archaic and individualised sense of nostalgia to rebel against the conformity of commercialisation. However, Anderson through [the framing] in his films [suggests] that the reality he constructs is one that cannot be [sustained] — and perhaps never [truly] existed in the first place. Thus, we enter this [fictional] world through [an artificial] facade, which is clearly a construction. Therefore The Grand Budapest Hotel reveals that while filmmakers can, and perhaps should, pursue a unique artistic direction — unburdened by commercialisation — the kind of beauty that this creates will only ever exist in art, [and] it is fleeting. The only way to experience an Anderson film [as intended] is in the [cinema], [making] his [vision] [distinctive], [and] also making his films short-lived.   Yet, Anderson's [later] film The French Dispatch reveals that there are limits to artistic expression — particularly in a commercialised landscape. The film is [an] anthology told through [a series of] visual vignettes that each mimic articles in [a] esteemed publication, the titular French Dispatch, which features anything from "fashion, food, the arts — high and low". [Still, one] film which reveals the [restraint] of style to be anything but 'barbaric' (as the statement puts it) is Hayao Miyazaki's The Wind Rises. Section C — Statement 14When works of art are created, [they inevitably involve an] alteration of reality. Literature claims: "this is not the real thing, but [a] depiction of it". Thus, literature always inhabits a zone [somewhere] between [simulated] reality that exists [in our minds] and the physical world. [Three] works of literature that explore these intersections and even breach the limits of language are Benjamín Labatut's essay collection When We Cease to Understand the World, Patricia Lockwood's No One Is Talking About This, and Hera Lindsay Bird's "Lost Scrolls".   Benjamín Labatut's When We Cease to Understand the World centres around scientific discovery — a field that arguably centres around objective truthfulness and reality — yet Labatut, through the language of literature, deliberately distorts the science that he writes about. In the essay [on] biographical details about Werner Heisenberg, [titled] "The Kingdom of Uncertainty", Labatut [tells a] story [where] one night while Heisenberg's breakthrough discovery of the uncertainty principle [was developing], he was wandering through a park, entirely intoxicated. Through [vivid] and dream-like imagery, Heisenberg's equation morphs from numerical particles into "glimmering parades of [particles]" and "countless [men] and women, their eyes slanted, their bodies sculpted from soot and ash". Labatut is aware that nobody — not even Heisenberg [himself] — can [fully] comprehend [the] mathematics, so he contextualises Heisenberg's equation, [allowing] it to be altered from reality and take the form of people, light, and explosions [that] we can [imagine]. [While] one [might not] grasp Heisenberg's uncertainty principle [on a purely] [mathematical] level, we feel its weight through [literature]. [The] language of dreams allows Labatut [to] communicate [a] more [profound] emotional truth. Thus When We Cease to Understand the World reveals a fundamental idea [about] reality [and] art. Literature [has long been] criticised for [distorting] reality, for [only] presenting fiction [rather than] reality. However, Labatut shows that humanity must be capable of simulating reality, of consciously entering the intersections between fact and fiction, as [this is] not only necessary, but an inescapable part of human nature. [It is how we] contextualise [and] understand the world.   However, two texts which create [blurring] between dreams and reality [through] language are No One Is Talking About This and "Lost Scrolls". Through [a kaleidoscopic] and ever-multiplying [stream] of imagery, both Patricia Lockwood and Hera Lindsay Bird create [poetic] [passages] that [hover between] physical [presence] and [nothingness]. In her book Actions and Travels: How Poetry Works, scholar Anna Jackson notes [a] trend in contemporary poets like Lockwood and Bird, where [vivid] imagery is used as [a form of] ornamentation to [evoke] an "airy nothing". These similes are paradoxical: [for instance,] "it's like a tornado in a harmonica" [and] "it's like loading a catapult with [a] [catapult]". With each new simile, the 'it' [that] the poem describes becomes harder to define. A similar technique is used in Lockwood's No One Is Talking About This, which opens with the line: "She entered the portal and the mind met her more than half-way there. Inside it was tropical and snowing". Lockwood's paradoxical imagery — 'tropical and snowing' — not only emulates the [abundance] of the internet (which the novel is centred around), but also becomes [pure] language [play]. By using oxymorons, [the] subjects of Lockwood and Bird's descriptions become [elusive] — [suggesting that] everything [is] nothing. [Yet] the [shift in tone] that [follows] the opulent imagery in [both works] [reveals] [a] [deep] sincerity. The second part of [Lockwood's] novel centres [around] her niece who dies [from a] genetic condition. [Here] Lockwood's [elaborate] [prose] is given [weight] [because it is] attached to something real: her sister's baby. While the imagery remains [dense], through descriptions like "[the baby] thought of lava-lamps and [clambering] over molasses, that sweet spread of information, that happened long ago in our minds", [even though] it is still hard to grasp what Lockwood is describing in the physical sense, those metaphors now hold [real] emotional weight [to them] as we come to understand that this child means everything to the speaker — it is not [mere] hyperbole. Similarly, "Lost Scrolls" also shifts [tone] and [tells us] that "this is what love is like"; in light of this [shift], the airy 'dreams' and similes that Bird uses take [on a] tone of sincerity, as we [recognise] [her vulnerability]. Though, unlike Lockwood, Bird's switch to [candour] recontextualises the language that came before. [The] poem's final line goes: "[last] night nobody came to our house... [and] murdered [us] in [our] sleep". While still characteristically hyperbolic, this line reveals Bird's use of imagery [to be] a [defence mechanism]. Bird [multiplies] [meanings] to avoid her [fear] of being in [a] relationship. In both cases, however, these authors juxtapose the 'dreams' of language [with] the [weight of] reality.
