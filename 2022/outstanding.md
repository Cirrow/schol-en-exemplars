Section A
When reading literature, we will often seek to make a connection [to our] own life. We see a character or persona [who] is similar to [us], and we understand or relate to [them in a] meaningful way. However, [it can be] difficult for people [from] marginalised communities who are under-represented to relate. Both Texts A and B [explore] the disconnect that these groups of people feel. Yet, Text A [by] Sam Brooks suggests that this connection can ultimately be found [through] acceptance in literature, while Text B [by Sia Figiel] believes a connection can be created, even if [only] to provide an escapist alternative from one's reality.

In Text A, Brooks highlights the difficulty readers from marginalised communities experience while reading literature. In particular, he focuses [on the] experiences of queer people. Using evocative [language], he compares [the experiences of] queer people and [the] mainstream [majority]. Here, a metaphor [compares] mainstream readers to people able to walk straight through the front door, whereas people from marginalised [communities] have to [climb down the] chimney to find a way into the house. This comparison [highlights how much] easier it is for "the mainstream" to access [literature] — they can simply walk straight in. The [experience of] literature is not the same for [people] who are under-represented in it; they are more likely to struggle to connect with it.

Similarly, in Text B, Figiel highlights [the] initial difficulty in connecting with literature when it does not reflect the reader. Figiel [writes about] reading [the poem] Daffodils [written by English poet William Wordsworth in 1802]. Figiel and Wordsworth are from two very different worlds in different cultures and a different time period. Because of this difference, [it is not surprising] that Figiel feels [an] initial disconnect from Wordsworth. Indeed, [like] Brooks, she is able to highlight [this] through language. [She recalls,] "I wandered lonely as a cloud / when I first heard [that] little poem". A simile compares [her] reading Daffodils to a cloud, floating in an empty sky. [This] serves to emphasise her isolation and ultimate lack of connection to the poem. [Later in the poem,] "a mango tree" is symbolic of [aiga /] Samoa. This emphasises the difference between Wordsworth's [culture] and Figiel's. [Both texts] highlight how initially when [we] read a text, it can be a difficult experience to connect with it.

Brooks, in Text A, feels that this connection to literature can ultimately be found. Rather than [feeling] defeated, he does not believe this to be a bad thing, but rather [uses metaphor to] convey the way [literature works]: "Stories aren't mirrors, they're windows". Figiel, in Text B, does also believe that reading literature can offer an insight into the writer's life, [and] certainly [that] it differs in that it becomes a sort of [escapism]. Typographically, the shape of the poem is wave-like, [which] gently [rises and falls]. [This] is contrasted by the fact [that] she feels [trapped] as she tries to find some [solitude]. The [image] of the "rain" has positive connotations [associated] with peace and serenity. [Yet] the [peacefulness] of the [rain] is disrupted by the asyndetic listing of [noises], [which] makes it feel chaotic and frantic. This [highlights] what is actually happening in Figiel's life. It suggests that since reading Daffodils is potentially stressful, [she finds] solitude [and] creates a connection with her [own world] because of the escapism from her life into Wordsworth's [poem]. The epistrophe of "precious" [and the] change in possessive adjective before it [signifies] that through escapism, Figiel [claims the] poem [as her own]. [Both] Texts A and B [agree] that [even] without representing [every reader], literature can provide a [meaningful] escape from reality. [They] agree that in [reading], there is [a] connection [to be found]. [However, they differ in how that connection works]: Brooks, in Text A, [suggests] that connecting with the [author's] life [is possible], and that it can only be [achieved] by [looking through windows]. Figiel, in Text B, [suggests] that after [initial alienation], a connection to the literature was [still] possible, [via] experiencing it as a form of escapism. [For Brooks, the] experience of reading literature is most important in understanding the author's world, while for [Figiel], it is important in providing a [sense of] escapism [from] one's own reality.
