Section A
The writers of the article Sam Brooks [reviews], James Courage Diaries, and [Sia Figiel's] poem The Daffodils from a Native's Perspective show [how readers] experience literature in a similar way, [but reach] different conclusions. On [the one hand], Sia Figiel [writes] her poem passionately describing the relationship she has formed with Daffodils, [as] a work [that offers escape]. [On] the other hand, Sam Brooks writes from [a place of] [critique], dismissing [the idea that readers must always] find [themselves in a text]. Instead, he dismisses the very thing that Sia Figiel is describing in her poem.

Text A sees Sam Brooks write [with a] [candid] but slightly cynical tone. He dismisses those who seek out relating literature to themselves, and twisting [a text] to see themselves in it. This tone, [both] cynical yet familiar, shows a writer not dismissing literature, but one who is attempting to guide his readers to what he believes to be the true meaning of the [act of] reading literature.

[On the other] hand, Sia Figiel uses [possessives] throughout [to create] an innocent and curious tone. It feels incredibly personal as [she] uses [possessives] [that give a] sense of entering her world through [references to] "my aiga", the village, [and] [ballet]. [She shows] that to her, the daffodils became [incredibly] personal to [her]. [The] author's [use of] "your precious daffodils, my precious daffodils, my only possession" shows [a] sense [of] [claim] through her claiming of the poem that is both [playful] and [defiant], and places us in the author's shoes. We are in Sia Figiel's [world], [watching her] find [meaning] in something so far removed from her circumstances. This [shows how] she [forms a personal] connection to [the poem].

Text A and B may view [texts] through different lenses, and [experience them] differently, but there is [a] middle ground to be found. The metaphor that Sam Brooks uses at the end of Text A, "Stories aren't mirrors, they're windows", [can] be flipped on [its] head by Text B. [In] Text B, [even though the] setting and innocent tone [are] different from the text she identifies with, [in] spite of [this], she finds something she can attach to and relate [to]. [There is] hope and [meaning] to be found. Literature according to [Brooks] calls [us] to not look so [hard] where there's nothing to be found. But what's the harm? Sia Figiel shows that literature is [alive] and powerful, while Brooks observes with a gentle cynicism. Section B — Statement 4
Novels have this unique and profound effect of leaving readers with [a lasting] image that drops [our] jaws and leaves us deep in thought. [One] profound effect of novels is the stopping of time, [where] that [final] moment [carries] [implicit] power [that we] observe before closing the book. The final moment we readers can [hold onto] when this moment is [so] potently profound [is when] the writer calls to us and tells us, "Let's not forget this!". Throughout the novels American Psycho by Bret Easton Ellis, The Stranger by Albert Camus, The Picture of Dorian Gray [by Oscar Wilde], and The Great Gatsby by F. Scott Fitzgerald, we observe endings, [particular] moments frozen in time, that compel [the] reader to never forget the image left in their heads and the messages that [moment] conveys. [Why] is this [effect] unique to novels though? [Because] other forms like poetry and short stories simply do not [have] the time to develop the [build-up] of that final frozen moment. Films and television [give us] a [rapid stream] of visual [images]. [By] the time [you] reach the end of a film you have [countless] frozen moments [in your mind].

Novels [leave us with] only the most profound moments that stick with us. We cannot forget that particular moment where time stops finally and is left in our heads because it is the culmination of all the images [that] come before it. But why are those particular moments [so] unforgettable? [In] the case of American Psycho, [there is] the stark contrast of [the ending]. [Patrick] Bateman has been murdering, [torturing], and [committing atrocities] throughout the novel. Often mistaken for his co-workers by [others], Bateman struggles to assert his identity. No matter how many reservations [he makes], [or] how many Armani suits he [wears], his hyper-consumerist outlook [leaves him feeling empty]. His [attempts at] personal expression [leave him] unable to [connect], as he describes [himself as feeling] hollowness and emptiness. In truth, his [violence is] a means of self-expression, to assert himself [as] different from all those that surround him. [While] the images [that] Easton Ellis describes throughout the novel are vivid, brutal, and grotesque, they are not what concludes the novel. When Easton Ellis chooses to stop time, he elects to do [so] at Bateman's confession to his lawyer, who, brutally and ironically, mistakes him for someone else. His lawyer proceeds to tell Bateman that the crimes [Bateman] described [could not have happened] and that Bateman would be incapable of hurting a fly. Bateman is left reeling [from the realisation that] his acts of atrocity have meant nothing. [His] confession has meant nothing. In other words, his evil has not been for nothing — he is [still trapped in his own hollowness], spite of his murderous spree. He seeks punishment not because of justice, but because he finally wants [to] be seen. Easton Ellis finishes [with] a brief scene of Bateman resuming his [routine] with colleagues, [where] time stops. It's unforgettable not because we have empathy for Bateman, but because we see the [hollowness] of a man with everything in the world but [no real] identity. Easton Ellis does this to show the emptiness that capitalism and consumerism bring to someone whose life [is built on them].

This kind of dichotomy between the majority of the text and its freeze-shot ending is common [in] [other] novels. In The Picture of Dorian Gray, we see the titular character, much like Bateman, build his life upon excess. His life philosophy [is] instilled into [him] by the domineering Lord Henry: "To cure the soul by means of the senses, and the senses by means of the soul". It is Dorian's [desire] to indulge and remain beautiful, [an] ability granted to him [by] the portrait [which ages in his place], [that shapes his life]. Throughout the novel, similar to The Great Gatsby and American Psycho, lavish decadence and materialism is [central]. His fixation on beauty and all things [aesthetic] is his way of [coping with] [isolation]. [Yet] he [begins to feel a] sense of isolation as those around him begin to reject him for his hedonism and lack of compassion. His [arc] parallels Bateman's, with them both clawing desperately at hedonism and materialism [as] the solution to their issues of isolation and disconnect. It is this that Oscar Wilde [undoes] at the end of the novel by having Dorian stab the painting, killing him and reverting his body back [to] its corrupt and decrepit self. This final image as the closing image of the novel is frozen with [Dorian dead on] the ground, as the portrait [is restored to its youth]. [It] shows the [downward spiral] of a man obsessed with himself and the pursuit of beauty. Wilde, much like Easton Ellis, is telling the reader to "not forget this" because it stands in stark contrast to [his earlier glamour], [delivering] warnings about the perils of materialism, self-obsession, and [moral decay].

This stopping of time need not reflect [a] downward spiral and punishment of an evil character, but [can] point, also, to a misguided protagonist with similar warnings to Dorian Gray and Patrick Bateman. The Great Gatsby shows the titular character Gatsby in his ever-enduring quest to [achieve] what can now be called the American Dream. Pining after Daisy Buchanan, and rising from poverty, reinventing himself as Gatsby, [whom] the narrator describes as [full of hope], [he is a] character set on taking his place at the top of society. [His] charming personality, and his insistence on [his dream] at all costs, ends with his murder. His carelessness and overvaulting ambition lead to his utter downfall. The [moment] frozen in time at the ending of the novel [is] Gatsby's funeral and Nick's description of its futility. No one attends the funeral, save a handful [of people], in stark contrast to the lavish parties [earlier in] the text, highlighting the [illusion] that [material wealth] really [matters] despite Gatsby's wealth. And the futility of Gatsby's dream — which Nick describes as [boats] "borne back ceaselessly into the past" — [makes] Gatsby's moment, much like Dorian Gray's and Bateman's, unforgettable because its message rings so clear. And that [message is] that materialism and ambition are nothing without human connection.

All three texts show characters misguided in their pursuits of fulfillment, [ending up] [empty]. [These] characters [are all] lacking one thing that would have lifted their miserable isolation: real human connection. These three texts each become so unforgettable because they freeze themselves in a particular moment and allow us, as readers, to reflect. These texts have used dichotomies of [excess and emptiness] to show [what] authors are [saying] about the human condition. The ability for novels to stop time, in [a] particular manner that only a novel can have, [makes a story's] messages regarding [the human condition] highly effective and memorable.

Section C — Statement 13
[The] ability for stories to [transcend] time [and] place [through] universality helps them to become dynamic forces for change in the real world. Often ingrained [in them are] warnings [about] human nature, allowing us, even today, to step back [and reflect]. So, although these stories [may seem] set [in another time], it is [their relevance] [that keeps them] forceful in the world of today. By examining the texts The Handmaid's Tale by Margaret Atwood, The Yellow Wallpaper by Charlotte Perkins Gilman, The Crucible by Arthur Miller, and The Day of the Sheep by Janet Frame, it is possible to see the power that these stories can have.

Many texts [create] imagined [or] historical worlds through which [they] express ideas. [We] can simply look to Aesop's Fables to find instances of simple moral [lessons] that continue to be [relevant]. The reason [these] stories can be [a dynamic] force is because their [depictions mirror] [real-world] scenarios. Look at The Crucible, which uses the historical world of the 17th-century Salem Witch Trials in Puritan New England as an allegory for McCarthyism. While McCarthyism [was a real phenomenon], its [themes and] messages [keep] Miller's [play] relevant today. During the trial of the play, [set around] the height of McCarthyism in [the 1950s], Judge Danforth, [who is] blinded by his faith and ideology, makes the statement: "You are either with this court or you must be counted against it, there be no road between". Within the justice system of Salem, everything [is black and white]. There is no middle ground, compromise, or nuance. Society in Salem is condemned through [this rigid thinking]. [This kind of] thinking doesn't just mirror the tyranny of McCarthyism, [but we can] look at the [modern] American situation in regards to the bi-partisan politics of the United States. The two-party system of the US aligns [people] either as a Republican or Democrat, and [demands either] support or vehement opposition to the current government. Miller's story [is not only a warning] of yesterday, but reminds us of [the divisions] of today, actively [calling on us] to look at ourselves. With many calling to end bipartisanship in American politics, it becomes clear that the lessons of the past in story must come forth again.

Likewise, Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale exists within a [fictional world] [that] reminds us [of] the fundamentalist [oppression] of the present day. Gilead, created by Atwood, was not [born] purely from her imagination. [When she] published the [novel] in 1985, Atwood [drew on historical examples]. Surrounded by [regimes] [and] [movements] where women were being [subjugated], [and where] many groups of radicals were calling for the reinforcement of traditional female roles in society, Atwood followed this to what she described as its "logical end" to create a brutal and oppressive society ruled by fear that places women firmly at the bottom of the societal hierarchy. Though written nearly [40] years ago, the text's messages [remain urgent] through the eyes of [the narrator] Offred, and her story shows the [enduring] effect that [such oppression] still [has] to this day. In nations [such as] Afghanistan, the ruling [regime] [enforces] incredibly strict restraints on women, demanding them to keep their entire bodies covered and to remain inside, [limiting their freedom] to vote, go to school, [or] speak up. While The Handmaid's Tale explored a [fictional] society, and Afghanistan [is a real place], [the pattern of oppression can be observed in both]. Storytelling is such a dynamic force for the very reason that [something] that was created [decades ago] [can still resonate]. Just as in The Crucible, [where] the [warnings remain] [relevant], the power of the story is [in] how universally it can be applied anywhere. At one point Offred asserts that [her body] "is a valuable possession". [With] statements [like this], the story allows us to put ourselves in the shoes of someone suffering under oppression. Stories like The Crucible and The Handmaid's Tale make [us feel] empathy more easily than the news does because [they can take] us inside the minds of people [and show] their lives and livelihoods, [rather than just] figures. [A] human story, even though it's fictional, moves us because it feels real.

But [what if] these stories hit closer to home, when they don't inhabit [extraordinary] worlds or circumstances, and when their characters are ordinary people facing monumental challenges? [In] Charlotte Perkins Gilman's The Yellow Wallpaper, [published in] 1892, the narrator's mental struggle after being prescribed a rest cure for postpartum depression reflects a time when psychiatry was still primitive. The birth of her daughter [triggered] [her] struggle with postpartum depression. After being prescribed a rest cure, [and] disallowed from reading or writing — the very things that drove her passions — for three months she was, as she later described, on "the brink of utter mental ruin". The Yellow Wallpaper, inspired by these experiences, condemns this rest cure. [The] narrator spirals into complete mental [distress], [with] her own husband subjecting her to his power as a man and [physician]. Gilman expresses this kind of ordinary circumstance (for the time), facing extraordinary mental anguish, to help warn people of the dangers of [repressive] social [norms] and lack of autonomy. The control that [her husband] [exerted] [over her] [is a critique of patriarchy]. [Over time], many stopped prescribing rest cures. [It] is the greater emphasis on ordinary human struggle and mental illness that makes this story such a forceful [work]. [Similarly, in Janet] Frame's The Day of the Sheep, a woman [is] living trapped by her surroundings, [where] the suburban [world feels] "so small and suffocating and untidy". Like in The Yellow Wallpaper, protagonist Nance struggles with the helplessness of her situation. [Watching] sheep in her washhouse, Nance [unknowingly] relates herself to the helplessness of [the] sheep, and the stream of consciousness and [fragmented] flow of the story reflects the repetitiveness of her days. She, like the narrator of The Yellow Wallpaper, is [caught in a] struggle where boredom and domesticity numbs the mind. This [portrays] a universal [truth].

[Ultimately], Gilman and Frame's depiction of mental illness [resonates with] the everyday. [They] show [what] millions of [people] who feel [trapped] [experience]. [These stories are important] because [they warn] us to look out for [those who may] not look like they are struggling. Stories offer a [voice for] people suffering [in silence]. [They act as a] dynamic force for good in the present day. This shared thread of struggle, anguish, and even [desperation] that these texts offer allows [us to] see inside the minds of people [in ways that] can be hard to understand from the outside. [Stories give] a voice to the voiceless. [They allow us] to assess them [and] [re-evaluate] the world around us today.
