Section A — Close reading of unfamiliar texts
Task: Compare the writers’ treatment of nature in Text A and Text B.
Candidate response
As humans in our modernized world, it can be easy to forget about the nature we are surrounded by. Text A and B have vastly
different stances of the presence and use of nature in our society are own lives today.
Nature is beautiful and must be cherished and therefore preserved. We like to think that as humans we are separate from nature, but
Keri Hulme shows us through an extract from One Whale Singing that we are more interconnected than we may expect. The
wonderous tone with which she writes is to connect with the reader to implore them to regard nature the same way she does by
approaching everything with curiosity and attention. "Diatoms of phantom light, stray single brilliances" conveys the beauty of nature
when you stop and be present and consider the smaller parts of the bigger picture. So caught up are we in our fast paced digital life,
that stopping for a moment allows us to discover the joys of what is right in front of us. We explore the idea of nature through the
third perspective of a watcher describing the whale, but we also hear descriptions of the whale's thoughts and feelings where she
can see "colours known only from dreams", adding not only to the awe the whale has towards us but also the awe we should have
towards the whale as we are different creatures but live in the same exact world. We can become complacent with the world we live
in, and taking the persepective of the whale allows us to see that everything is exciting if we choose to embrace it that way. This
spirtuality and connectedness with nature is again explored through the "memories that are bone deep” in the whale. The idea of
these memories integrated into our very being is a notion that is significant in many of our cultures as humans, where our legacy and
our place on earth are only due to the ancestors that came before us. Towards the end of the text we feel a tone shift as the killing of
whales is described. The female whale "mourning for a male" as he dies in "threshing agony" as the barbs "sunk into his flesh by the
heart" are highly emotive uses of language to support Hulme's point that other creatures also feel, this is not something unique to

humans. Hulme emphasises that not only do we live in the same world as these graceful creatures, we are also similar with them in
many ways through our innate curiosity, sociability, and emotions.

It is undeniable, however, that our capability for the manipulation of nature is what led to us being here today. In Women love me,
fish fear me, Jordan Hamel explores the necessity of the ocean for our way of living, however he does so through an entirely
different tone and perspective than Hulme does. While Hulme focusses on the interconnectedness of humans and nature, Hamel
focusses on the uses of nature as his career as a fisherman who has been "trawling this shoreline for centuries". His matter of fact,
humorous tone serves as a stark contrast to Hulme's deeply emotional response to nature. "I often dream of the creature from The
Shape of Water, I bet he tastes amazing with vinegar" shows the lack of seriousness with which Hamel regards the fishing industry.
Fish are simply regarded as food to eat or sell to others, and to him a human shaped fish would be just as good or better for this
cause. Hamel's reasons for these absurd ideas is clear as we see through his listing that this is his way of lifestyle and is how he
supports him and his family. Fishing we see is even a way to connect with others as he passes down his knowledge to his children
on "how the seaweed slips through sand" or how "light refracts on the water”. The skill and experience the narrator has does invoke
a sense of appreciation for the art and skill of fishing that the narrator has. Hamel displays how fishing is a large source of food for us
and we should feel thankful for the ones who source this food. This is especially as we even feel a sense of nostalgia as the narrator
describes being out in the sea "roughhousing in the dingy", even fishing can be fun with the right people.

The biggest difference between Text A and Text B is that in Text B Hamel largely sees nature as something to be monetised and
used whereas in Text A Hulme sees nature as something to be preserved and carefully considered. They both are able to achieve
this through their different tones, with Hulme having a tone filled with wonder and excitement but also sorrow of the, whereas
Hamel's tone is highly humorous and not meant to be regarded as serious.

While I believe both texts are valuable for exploring the different aspects of nature, I believe that Text A was a more effective text
because of its emotive diction that enabled me to gain a new sense of appreciation for nature. Text B showed me a more humorous
and light hearted way to make sense of your career and use of nature, but Text A had a much bigger impact on my understanding of
our interconectedness with nature as something to be cherished.

Candidate response
The beauty of short story lies in its ability to focus on and explore one sole aspect of literature rather than fleshing out an entire
world, characters, and plot. Short stories act as a snapshot in time, deliberately leaving the reader with often more questions than
answers. This ambiguity are simply the pieces, and like a jigsaw puzzle, the reader is left to reflect on the story and its relevance to
their own lives to put the pieces together and construct their own understanding of the text.
One joy of short stories is its ability to focus purely on one character and their journey in a condensed timeframe. Flowers for
Algernon by Daniel Keyes utilises the short story format in order to show a sharp change in order to make the reader feel for the
narrator, Charlie Gordon. Charlie is a mentally disabled adult and is the subject of an experiment to triple his intelligence. It is only
through his epistolary diary recounts and the brevity of his story that Keyes’ true impact shines through. From a shift in his
understanding of the world, Charlie initially just "wants to be smart” as he aspires to be like those around him so that he will be liked-
before discovering that his new intelligence only isolates him from others. Devastatingly, Charlie understands that "everybody
laughs" at him because of the mistakes he makes but now that he's intelligent "everyone seems to be frightened" of him because
they are unsettled by his change and that he is now more intelligent than themselves. Keyes' short story is to question soceity's
treatment of other people, and the importance we place on intelligence rather than human compassion and care as Charlie
comments that now he only "wants to be loved". Flowers for Algernon deliberately does not develop or describe the world that
Charlie lives in as the relevant takeway is his journey and what it has shown us about the need for connection and the harshness
with which we target those behind their backs. For a short story, extensive world building is not necessary to get the point across. We
never find out what happens to Charlie after the experiment begins fails and his mind and body deteriorates as the story abruptly
ends before that fully happens. This is an intentional choice by Keyes in order to make us reflect on the short experience. In that
way, short stories are just like out own lives where we do not have the answers to everything but can only be presented with a small
part of the story and figure out how the pieces fit together on our own terms.
Short stories do not even need to have plot development in order to be effective at spreading a message. They do not need to have
clear worldbuilding established. In The Ones Who Walk Away From Omelas by Ursula K Le Cruin, the setting of the short story is
kept extremely vague and there is no sembelance of a typical story structure with the expected conflict and resolution, unlike Flowers
for Algernon which still has a plot. In The Ones Who Walk Away From Omelas we are simply presented with a concept. Omelas is a
supposed utopia where everybody there is happy, yet we aren't even explicitly told what makes Omelas so special. Written in second
person, the narrator tells the reader that it's best if Omelas is "imagined as your own fancy bids". Le Cruin's point isn't about what an
ideal world is, but it is to criticize those who believe that an ideal world even exists. Because nothing good ever comes without a
cost. The nature of the short stories allow for a drastic shift of tone from the wonderful world of Omelas to the basement, a "room
with no windows and the door is locked" that houses a child. This child is a contrast to the adults of Omelas, who are supposedly
"mature, intelligent and passionate" whereas the child is an "imbecile through fear, neglect, and malnutrition". The stark shift in tone
and contrast between an adult who should know better and an innocent child is powerful social commentary on taking advantage of
those less fortunate than ourselves. Because "everybody [in Omelas] understands that their happiness depends wholly on this child's
abominable suffering", yet they still choose to stay for their own gain. Le Cruin criticizes the complacency with which our developed
countries and privileged individuals fall into acts of consummerism and turning a blind eye when it doesn't suit us. From the clothes
we wear through fast fashion and sweat shops and the products we buy from underpaid child labour, we are all guilty of enabling
suffering. In order to do so, the world of Omelas is deliberatly kept vague so that it is easily applicable to our own. However, we are
always presented with a decison. The last line of the short story reveals that some of the citizens of Omelas walk away "into the
darkness" and never return again. Like in Flowers for Algernon, the ending is not fleshed out so that we know what happens
afterwards. We have no idea what happens to these people who leave and where they go, and if the child ever makes it out of the
basement. This is entirely Le Cruin's point as it is not about the plot and story, it is about the concept and the ideas it shows us and
we are meant to take the pieces and reflect on ourselves and our actions in society.
Short stories are a unique form of literature because they do not require to be fully fleshed out in order to have in impact. In fact, it is
often the texts that are deliberately kept vague and open ended that leaves the audience to think deeply about one concept.
Whereas longer texts may have more events and tribulations, short stories allow for the sole focus on one idea in order to fully
communicate the author's point and as a result, enable the reader gain a further understanding of our world.

Candidate response
Everything in life is a balance. Joy and goodness simply can not exist without pain and suffering so both need to be present.
Literature is a medium that acts as a mirror to our own lives as works of texts are carefully crafted by author's from their own
thoughts and experiences. It is therefore through literature that we can see both humanity at its best and at its worst, and understand
why we feel pain in order to make room for seeking out joy.
If humans should be capable of goodness, why do so many inflict pain onto others? Why do we so easily turn to violence over
reason? The power of literature lies in its ability to help us understand the complex and nuanced nature of humans and as a result
our treatment or rather mistreatment of others. Joker (2019) explores Arthur Fleck's rise into the classic DC villain Joker. Joker acts
with total anarchy, violence, and chaos with no apparent reason or care for any of his actions, but this film gives insight into his real
motives. The city of Gotham is falling into ruin and this is no more obvious than through the contrast in settings between the elite
wealthy and the struggling lower class. Whereas the wealthy areas are lush, ordered, and neat, the lower class live in a dirty city with
broken infrastructure, garbage and rats, and violent youth. And here is where we begin to make understanding of the pain and
struggle of humans, as like in our own society, in Gotham classism is prominent and continues to prevail. A common belief by the
priviliged is that "those who have made something of our lives will always regard those who haven't as nothing more but clowns",
that they have worked hard enough to get where they are, even though the lower class are in a capitalist society that ensure to
benefit only the already wealthy and leave the rest struggling and static. From this idea we not only make sense of why Joker acts
the way he does, but also what is deeply painful to the human- being treated like dirt while others blame your character rather than
sympathise with your circumstance. So in a world that is full of this kind of suffering, it is a wonder where we can still find joy. For the
Joker, we see that his joy is not through having compassion for those that wronged him, but it is in finding a sense of community and
banding together for a shared cause. While yes, Joker sets the city on fire with riots in the street, we find that this is his source of joy
in life, finding his own absurdist purpose and connecting with other people even if it means inflicting harm. By making sense of the
root of suffering, Joker (2019) shows that the source of joy in life is not necessarily through compasison but rather about feeling like
you belong and have value as an individual.
Oftentimes, we like to believe that we are fair and just creatures. Oftentimes, we place ourselves on a pedastal above other
creatures, and believe we should be free from scrutiny. This false belief only inflicts pain onto others. In Frankenstein by Mary
Shelley, this arrogance is prevalent through the scientist Victor Frankenstein who toys with the creation of life by forming a creature
out of human body parts before immediately abandoning him. The creature, while brought into life infant-like and harmless with a
"confusion of senses" is met with abject horror and disgust from his own creator who is "unable to remain in the same room with
such a wretch." This unwarranted pain of rejection is enough to make anybody turn off other people. However, the creature, who
displays more humanity than his creator, still initially seeks out the love and attention from other people to show that, like in Joker
(2019), this is at our core what we need. It is his altruistic tendencies of "taking the tools" to gather resources for an impovershed
family that we find one of the sources of joy in humanity. Helping other people. This, however, doesn't last. The creature is constantly
met with terror and violence simply upon sight and, unable to seek out the connection he desires, he eventually becomes the
monster that they fear him to be. The pain of the creature can be used to relate to the pain we feel in our own society where
prejudices such as racial or gender stereotypes only force people into these boxes until they end up becoming these stereotypes.
Not because they want to, but because they have no other option that people will allow them to exist as. Because as the creature
notes, "if I cannot inspire love, I will create fear". It is only through this text that we are able to see the source of joy being at its root
an innate desire to be understood, and the sense of pain we feel when this is not attainable.
Literature serves to provide an outlet from which we as the consumers are able to relate to characters and their experiences. It is
through both their pain and their joy that we are able to make understanding of our own pain and joy and where these stem from.
Through this we are able to reflect on our bias and mistreatment of other people, as well as the fundamental care and connection we
need to experience in our lives, as to be human is to desire love.
