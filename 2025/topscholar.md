
# Section A

In "One whale singing" by Keri Hulme and "Women love me, fish fear me" by Jordan Hamel, the issue of fishing is explored. The writers highlight structure and sensory language to portray creatures being hunted. Where these texts differ is in their exploration of fishing and nature as a hobby with Text B presenting homecoming, a connection and a rite of passage for the persona. In contrast, Text A highlights the destructive nature of fishing, notably the excessive whaling of whales, using diction and sensory language to acknowledge the severe natural impact. While each writer notes the effect on the natural world, they differ in the focus and consequences, presenting the reader with alternative perspectives on fishing and nature.

This extract from Hulme's short story "One whale singing" uses structural techniques to increase the emotional impact on the reader, creating tension and uncertainty when harming nature. Text A begins with an exploration of the surrounding environment, following the typical narrative structure of a short story. The description of the environment develops and the climactic action of another whale being poached follows, before the extract closes with the female whale leaving the scene. This structure allows the reader to follow the whale's natural journey and form an attachment with the whale before being confronted with the reality of the situation, and thereby coming to understand the severity of whaling. At the beginning of Text A, short, simple sentences are used to provide a clear portrayal of the natural surroundings, while creating the calm, simple environment in an ocean that remains undisrupted by fishing. It reads, "A high burst of dolphin sonics. The school was returning. A muted rasp from shoalfish worrying past." This structural device allows Hulme's perspective on the value and beauty of nature to shine through. It is clear that to this writer, the issue of whaling and environmental disruption is not complex, but rather simple—a disappointing, unnecessary fact as reflected in the simple structure. Text A highlights the unnatural position of poachers infiltrating and disturbing the lives of sea animals. In separate paragraphs, and noted by parenthetical structures, Hulme portrays the unwelcome voice of fishermen in this scene. She writes, "(Lookitthar!)" and "(Getoutahere its comingin)" with unnatural syntax, not allowing a breath between words to create the perspective of disrupted sea creatures. The confusion that Hulme creates with this structural arrangement reflects the confusion and unnatural position that the fishermen create, providing commentary on the influence of mankind in nature, and firmly suggesting that fishing does more harm than good when the focus is placed on those inhabiting nature.

In contrast to Text A, Hamel's poem, "Women love me, fish fear me" utilises contrasting structures to portray fishing as a rite of passage for the persona, placing a focus on what nature can do for humankind rather than the impact humankind has on nature. While Hulme used short sentences to create a calm, natural effect, Jordan Hamel's use of complex, longer sentences portrays the special bond and natural connection the persona feels with fishing and nature. The poem reads, "You try to teach kids the little things in this life; / you try to show them how seaweed slips through / sand and snags on rocks!" This more complex syntactical arrangement allows the reader to experience the natural bond the persona describes through the smooth rhythm created by the enjambed lines coupled with the sibilance in "seaweed slips... sand and snags". These structural devices differ from Text A because while Hulme intends to portray the human presence in nature as disruptive and out of place, Hamel uses the poem's structure to present the connection between nature and humankind as inevitable and intertwined, highlighting how Text A and Text B differ in their treatment of nature.

Furthermore, the sensory language utilised in Text B aims to portray the natural and memorable times the persona spends in nature. While the persona would fish and have hobbies, the images created for the reader are designed to portray the persona's hobby as special and almost sacred. Hamel writes of the harmful effects of fishing before ironically commenting on the effect consumption of sea creatures. The poem notes, "the dolphins that get swept up in tuna nets... poached in a mercury brine," appealing to the reader's sense of taste and challenging the perspective that fishing is beneficial for humankind. This is repeated as the persona reminisces, suggesting he "move(s) through life / with a bucket of bait in one hand / and a beer in the other, all smiles and salt breath". By presenting this clear and familiar image to the reader, Hamel explores the positive memories that accompany fishing for the persona. The sensory language, such as smells and tastes evoked in the depiction of "a bucket of bait" and "a beer", attempt to provide relatability for the reader, highlighting the value of fishing for the persona, and presenting once again the relationship between humans and nature as closely interlinked and inextricably connected.

While in Text B, sensory language is used to highlight the fond memories of the persona and provide relatable, touching images for the reader, Hulme uses powerful sensory language to offer a poignant portrayal of the impact of man's influence in nature. Describing the female whale, the extract reads, "the sounds that came to her were thin and distorted" and the confusion of the whale is depicted once more. The description of the sounds as "thin and distorted" allows the reader to feel a similarly uncomfortable and disturbed feeling as the whale because while it is unclear what the whale truly hears, the sensory language used confronts the reader with the disruption and unnatural presence of humankind in nature. Furthermore, the portrayal of the sounds as "The sound waves, popped and spattered... into deeper water," introduces the harsh and severe impacts of humankind in nature with the plosive /p/ and dental /t/ and /d/ creating the violent and unnatural influence. The sensory language and images created, coupled with the sound devices, allows Hulme to present a poignant portrayal of the whale and note the harmful influence of humankind on nature.

Moreover, Text A uses figurative language to delve deeper into the whale's experiences and note the violence and destruction that fishing and human involvement has in nature. Hulme writes that the light brought up memories that were "bone deep in her, written in her very cells," using metaphorical language to portray the natural life and experiences of the whale and the juxtaposing disruption of the fishing boat. Here, the idea of the knowledge of land being "bone deep" presents the interconnection between natural creatures and their habitats while highlighting the disruption of humankind in this bond. As the short story goes on, the climax of the male whale being killed by a harpoon presents a final, poignant image of the destructive, but also the murderous aspect of humankind in this poaching. Hulme writes, "the whale blew red in his threshing agony" presenting the reality of the situation to the reader, noting the brutality of the killing. This shows Hulme's perspective clearly, using visceral imagery as they strongly criticise the severity of humankind's interference in nature. While not necessarily personification, the humanising of these two whales shows the effect of figurative language on the reader, increasing the emotional impact of fishing on the reader and highlighting the brutality.

In contrast to Hamel, Text A personifies the whales, using sensory and figurative language to portray their raw, painful experiences. However, Text B shows the persona talking casually over the fish they discuss killing. As the poem reads, "Life's too short to eat bowfin / or that minced pollack;" a stark contrast between Text A and B is highlighted. While Text A wrote about the animals as alive and being killed, Text B presents the animals as dead and to be eaten, a clear difference demonstrating the perspectives of the writers on nature and its value to humankind. The cliched idiom, "Life's too short" offers an ironic sentiment as the persona describes trying to go vegetarian but struggling given their love for fish and fishing. Hamel uses hyperbolic listing of language and polysyndetic listing to explain why he struggles to give up fish: "Hauling this shoreline for centuries, just me and my rod and my net and my two little sons." That Hamel uses this plethora of reasons indicates the persona feels connected to fishing, noting time, memories, and family, showcasing his view on nature and the interconnection between his memories and being in nature.

The diction and connotations of language in Text A and Text B highlight the thematic differences and demonstrate how the view of nature as something valuable is different between writers. In Text B, Hamel uses blunt, frank lexis to depict the fish he hunts, writing "frozen squid chunks," "too-snapper" noting the meat. His perspective on fish as a commodity, something to be consumed, is evident. Whereas [Hulme's] portrayal of the violence towards the whales shows a differing perspective on the impact of fishing on nature. The extract reads, "killing ships of the harpooners lurk" with violent connotations, highlighting the destructive force of mankind on nature. The diction of "lurk" once again notes the aforementioned unnatural presence of humankind in nature, with connotations of creeping and of uneasiness. While the end of Text B shows the persona acknowledge he "has so much love to give," [this] may seem touching. Yet when contrasted with the tone of sympathy created by the lexis in Text A, this love may be tainted with irony and the notes of violence that come from fishing. By ending with the "sweet night air," Hulme ends the extract with a hopeful tone, creating an optimistic atmosphere that still highlights the destruction that has taken place. Yet in a similar way to Text B, Hulme chooses to end with a warm and touching mood because each writer ultimately views nature with appreciation. It is the focus and method that differs.

Altogether, Text A and B can be compared based on their use of language and structure to portray the writers' perspectives on nature. While each similarly explores the natural world with ideas about gratitude and value, they differ in why. Text B explores how nature offers a present connection for the persona, creating opportunities for memories and bonds. Whereas Text A condemns the severe impact of humankind on nature, presenting the reader with a violent scene and urging the reader to acknowledge that when nature and the environment is concerned, mankind does more harm than good.

# Section B

Honoured for centuries as the world's greatest playwright, William Shakespeare's dramatic landscape presents society with an honest and realistic portrayal of humankind that psychologists and philosophers alike cannot help but marvel at. From comedies to tragedies, no play can convincingly challenge the absurdity that "Shakespeare was not a deep thinker". In *Hamlet*, a poignant portrayal of a man driven to madness due to grief and duplicity is presented. Through his depiction of Hamlet's descent into madness, Shakespeare highlights the psychological impact of loss and facades, with the question presented of what is madness within the supernatural and natural worlds. The audience of Shakespeare's plays are left more than satisfied by the beauty of his language, mesmerized by the validity and equally poignant confrontation as he echoes reality through his characters, challenging stereotypes through his plays.

To suggest that Shakespeare "was not a deep thinker" would be to seriously undervalue the works of playwrights to come, with the likes of greats like Arthur Miller and Samuel Beckett crediting the excellence of their plays to the genius of Shakespeare's plays. The intertextuality and metafiction aspect of *Hamlet* provides clear evidence to suggest Shakespeare was a "deep thinker." As Hamlet is desperate to verify the ghost's claim and reveal the duplicity of King Claudius, he employs players to act in his play within a play, ending his second act with the rhyming couplet, "The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king."

The statement appears to call into question Shakespeare's complexity and the depth of the themes and plots in his plays, understating not only their undeniable social impact and lingering influence in literature today, but also the value of the plot on its immediate, traditional audience. Shakespeare's metafiction with the play within a play in *Hamlet*, facades in *Twelfth Night*, and magical realism in *A Midsummer Night's Dream* highlight the transcension of this deep thinker. His ability to challenge the conventions of drama have allowed him to remain the most renowned playwright in history. Oscar Wilde wrote of the unique nature of Shakespeare's plays, noting "the eye as well as the ear were gratified." Shakespeare was not contrived by the limits of stagecraft, the restrictions on lighting, costuming or staging. Instead, as Wilde references, the descriptions spoken by his characters allowed his plays to transcend not only what was expected but what was possible. With the setting of *Hamlet*, characters describe the "bitter cold" as "the air bites shrewdly", demonstrating Shakespeare's genius. If the stage could not portray his vision, the dialogue of the characters would.

Furthermore, the aforementioned lasting impact on literature cannot be misrepresented. While the thinking that he was not "a great psychologist or a subtle philosopher" is clearly limited in its failure to address that every novel, poem, short story remain undoubtedly influenced by Shakespeare even today. As critic David-Everett Blythe surmised, an audience would "be startled that a Tennessee Williams character appeared to be speaking verbatim Shakespeare." The value of Shakespeare's language, the poetic nature of his dialogue and the beauty of his sonnets demonstrate that "what he was, was a very great poet." However, to disregard his societal impact on literature and language is nothing short of naive.

The portrayal of Hamlet's descent into madness challenges the assertion that Shakespeare was not "a great psychologist or a subtle philosopher." Gertrude describes Hamlet as "Mad as the sea and wind, when both contend / which is mightier." This line demonstrates the poetry of Shakespeare's dialogue in verse through the /w/ sound creating eerie winds of change. But also, the devastating image of Hamlet's depression and madness as described by his mother shows the touching depth of Shakespeare's writing as he fills crowds of people wanting to watch a story in which the audience is powerless to help Hamlet. In her book, *This is Shakespeare*, Emma Smith explores the effect of King Hamlet's purgatory. She writes, "The tragedy of regicide has made clear that the past preceded the play," beginning Hamlet from meeting his father. As Hamlet is prevented by heaven from killing Claudius, he cries, "O, that this too too solid flesh would melt, / Thaw and resolve itself into a dew!" in response to his father's ghost's call to avenge him. Here, Shakespeare's value as "a subtle philosopher" is evident as he introduces the inability to escape guilt and purgatory, challenging his characters' actions and urging the audience not to meet the same fate.

Hamlet is unable to avenge his father without himself committing regicide and subjecting himself to the same purgatory that the supernatural ghost of his father haunts him for. In his 1910 analysis of *Hamlet*, Freud addressed the political nature of Hamlet's fate, writing "the loathing which should have driven him to revenge is replaced by self-reproach... he himself is no better than the murderer whom he is required to punish." Considered one of the greatest psychologists of the 20th century, Freud's analysis provides evidence in alignment with the argument that Shakespeare explored the human mind more aptly than any writer to come. His works balanced the exploration of the mind. Audiences remain reflected and challenged in the 21st century as Shakespeare's plays infiltrate the thinking of students and question the assumptions of viewers wherever they witness his genius.

The hopelessness portrayed in Shakespeare's plays offer a catharsis to the audience as they witness the devastation and despair of characters like Hamlet. From the first act, Hamlet is warned not to follow the Ghost and repeat Claudius' crime. Horatio queries, "What if it tempt you toward the flood, / my lord, or to the dreadful summit of the cliff..." demonstrating Shakespeare's ability to balance psychological analysis with the comforting and entertaining relationships he writes, such as that between Hamlet and Horatio. The exploration of Hamlet's psyche is evident through dialogue like "You cannot take from me anything that I will not more willingly part withal—except my life" with similar ideas of hopelessness and depression explored in *Macbeth*, remembering that life's a tale "told by an idiot." Shakespeare's portrayal of his characters shows the effect of his writing on modernly described mental feelings. A modern audience can recognise the feelings described by characters like Hamlet as depression, suicidal ideation, and appreciate Shakespeare's position as a "great psychologist" while remaining comforted and challenged by the beautiful, poetic language he expresses these themes with.

Ultimately, to suggest the absurdity that "Shakespeare was not a deep thinker or a great psychologist or a subtle philosopher" is a naive attempt to summarise the thematic value of Shakespeare's work and reduce his societal impact to beautiful poetry. While Shakespeare's style and language remain one of the most significant shapers of literature today, the remarkable examinations of the human psyche and society determine that he transcended the drama genre and presented his audiences with powerful portrayals of mankind and what it means to love, not to love, and ultimately, to love.

# Section C

"For life to be felt as noble, it must be seen as tragic." Spoken by Eugene O'Neill to explain his poignant and violent play *Long Day's Journey into Night*, these words offer one side of the coin, that literature requires discomfort and expresses a pain so deep it evokes gratitude. The idea that "the twin purposes of literature are to make sense of pain and to fathom the sources of joy" provides an accurate assessment of some of the most enduring literature. Yet there is an argument to be made that pain is not necessary to reach joy. Though O'Neill would protest there is "a quality of understanding born only in pain," literature from many genres reminds us that joy does not have to sprout from a garden of pain, but where sunshine can be source enough.

Literature endures, remains impactful and memorable for numerous reasons, including the thematic value and moral impact on the reader or consumer. Taking Khaled Hosseini's portrayal of political control and gendered violence in Afghanistan, vivid descriptions of domestic violence towards characters in *A Thousand Splendid Suns* confront the reader with the severity of the situation for these women. It is interesting to question whether Hosseini asks his readers to "make sense of pain" suggesting an explanation for violence against women, or whether his novel is utilised to spread awareness and live in the pain of these women while we sit in its pages. Similarly, Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet* offers its audience the fate of its "star-crossed lovers" from its opening. There is no chance for hope and the love shared between these characters cannot save them from meeting death while still young. Yet for each of these texts, the purpose is not mitigated because it is hard or difficult to "fathom the sources of joy"—these themes are still prevalent, lessons are still given. Describing making sense of pain and fathoming joy as "twin purposes" would suggest close alignment, almost an inability to exist without the other, yet some literature like *A Thousand Splendid Suns* and *Romeo and Juliet* do not offer comfort or joy. This does not, however, take away their value.

The connection between joy and the pain can be seen when a writer offers comfort and solution to pain felt. Taking Billy Joel's "We Didn't Start the Fire" which provides its listener with a myriad of crises and leaders and events that shaped world history, while reminding them of pain and not shying away from the value of acknowledging struggles. Yet, the album *Storm Front* as a whole notes the need to sit in pain, while in turn presenting the listener with a moment to look to the past and thereby find joy together in the present. Similarly, Harper Lee's *To Kill A Mockingbird* presents the poignant resolution following a guilty verdict. Lee highlights that racial prejudice is ongoing—a bleak yet realistic reality—yet using Scout's character and her curiosity and willingness to question racism and prejudice, the novel's readers can find some comfort that while the verdict was unjust today, through a willingness to learn, [it] may be just tomorrow.

"Pain" here indicates true hardship and despair. Yet in literature, reflecting on [joy] can be enough to fathom sources of joy. In her poem, "Being Boring", Wendy Cope explores the simple joys that can develop from a simple, boring life—such as her garden growing. Here, the challenge the persona faces does not cause a sense of pain but merely slight unhappiness, a boring life. It is literature like this where the obstacle characters face is not pain for the writer nor the reader where we can question how closely aligned pain and joy need to be to create purposeful literature. Cope presents a stark contrast to Hemingway's "A Clean, Well-Lighted Place" in which Hemingway explores suicidal ideation and the judgement of others. In this short story, characters question why a man would want to kill himself if he has money and privilege. Hemingway provides an excellent contrast to the idea that through pain, we can find joy, as his character lives a traditionally "good" life, comfortable, living and thriving in a "clean, well-lighted place"—yet that has not saved him from feeling pain.

Ultimately, the idea that pain and joy are the "twin purposes of literature" is limited in its discussion on the value a text brings to its consumer. While literature often endures because of its complex transformation between the negative and the resolved, a text's purpose is not mitigated when it does not follow this conventional narrative structure. Instead, by blurring the lines between the sources of pain, sources of joy and the relationship they share, writers remind their consumers that joy can be found without needing to endure pain, while questioning whether joy can always be found at all.
