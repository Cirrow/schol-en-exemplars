### Section A — Statement 3

When someone mentions 'perseverance', my mind goes immediately to sport, and mental wellbeing. Both Young & Ireland explore both of these aspects respectively, with their own unique voice, evaluating on how the overall goal of perseverance is to find comfort in fulfilment. The overcoming of a challenge is fundamental in perseverance, and will always, as a final result, end in a type of peace, & fulfilment.

Young's 'On any walk' vividly depicts the physical aspect of perseverance, that of 'pushing on'. The writer immediately provides us with a scene riddled with negatively connotative adjectives (in the context of tramping) such as 'dense bush', and a juxtaposition of the comfort of not doing such, like 'hot water' and 'flushing toilet'. These luxuries that frequently recur whilst in non-optimal environments remind us of the possibility of turning back. The internal monologue (not explicitly annotated) in the first paragraph reflects the idea of the two creatures on either shoulder, both disagreeing with each other, telling the person opposite things to do, a visualisation of an 'internal conflict' metaphor, which has been extended across this prose piece. Whilst Young visualises internal conflict in a more uplifting environment, poet Kevin Ireland delves into the depths of depression & loneliness that comes with being human. The enjambment from the first to second line emphasises the lack of clear flow that the poem & poet both share. This poem reminisces about the past, which the poet struggles to comprehend with losses, using a tram metaphor for more close things such as family & friends. Whilst Young explores 'pushing on', the reciprocal 'letting go' also emerges as the opposite pole of the perseverance element, as discussed by Ireland. The mysterious setting is not so quickly established, but in the 'morning sky's strange cast', we can interpret that the mental status of the poet is in a strange state, likely since the loss of something dear, like the trams. This is commonly seen in more elderly people, their perseverance to not let go of something symbolic to their past, either personal, or family/friend related, which helps comfort & remind them of something else. Ireland writes the poem with good meaning, but also evaluates on it—the harm that can [come from it]—self-awareness (negative), and loneliness in the form of depression.

Both Young & Ireland, however, show the emotional responses of such perseverance. The idea that it pushes you outside of your comfort zone is clearly evident in 'On any walk'. That growth requires discomfort is not merely a myth, but unwaveringly true. The fear of failure, of losing motivation, is what motivates the author to push on & continue. They see themselves nearer the end of the poem as resulting in [pushing] on. This shows one should push themselves in all aspects of life, to their extent, and beyond, as only then can one discover & harness that the potential abilities, initially not evident to them. Young may be referencing the fact that the biggest burden to ourselves, is ourself, especially when we are in self-conscious scenarios. Young may wish for us to look & see that humans deep within ourselves are narcissistic, egotistic, selfish. Really, not too many people care to judge us, and therefore perseverance should similarly be focused on marshalling our own motivation to continue & push on to reach goals, which always seems to be a type of peace in different contexts. Ireland also explores this emotional response to fear in his poem, but that being the fear of loss. The continuous enjambment disrupts the rhythmical clarity of the extract, making it somewhat harder to read, and requiring the reader more perseverance as well to fully grasp the ideas of the piece. This enjambment also reflects the fragments of memory coming back to the poet slowly and not in any systematic sections, reflects the affect of reminiscing (especially for case studies of such dementia, etc.). The perseverance it requires to overcome such fear of losing valuable & treasured memories, as is depicted beautifully as 'steamy windows', 'cloudy days', and 'having lost their destination signs', is incredible, and a personally massive achievement in itself.

Both Young & Ireland look at the use of the fear emotion to persuade oneself to persevere, however both in their respective mediums of pushing on & letting go/holding on. We see the real reason for perseverance is more peace than discomfort, but requires much of it to achieve the goal. Both writers have left their poems/posts unfinished, not evaluating on the result of perseverance, signifying that it is a personal feeling of fulfilment, calling us to action. Not only Young, the Waitangi Treaty protests conveyed this perseverance, representing Aotearoa in our strive for peace & justice, by all people, regardless of ethnicity or background. Only in perseverance can we as people, a nation, and the world, unite, and in society, create peace & harmony with one another.

### Section B — Statement 2

Poetry has always layers of meaning, whether intended by the poet or not. Too often do teachers & students alike look deep into poetry, far beyond what purpose they are intended to have. Perhaps we should look at poems with new perspectives, childlike, when poetry is [simple], we should savour it, not destroy the freedom & variety this genre brings to us. However, what can simplicity be? Or childlike? Simplicity could be the lack of depth, or it could be the inability for readers to find intended depth. As readers, we can only try to interpret intentions, whether far beyond or nowhere near what has been provided. However, all poetry has been written by a poet in a [certain] state, and therefore, we should savour it by its ability to allow us to find unique perspectives on what they [mean].

William Carlos Williams' 'The red wheelbarrow', one of extreme simplicity, but is often analysed & taught to the extent of writing an entire essay per stanza. Why?? "so much depends / upon // a red wheel / barrow glazed with rain / water // beside the white / chickens." This poem, was it intended just to depict a red wheelbarrow and chickens? Then teachers ask us, 'why is it red?', just like 'why is the door red?' in Shakespeare. We should savour our lack of depth that is usually a result of a first reading. The massive array of poems over many centuries also depict a massive array of personal feelings, depression, suicide, [or] the way to love which shall prosper. Should poetry be treated as it has been written, like a blank canvas with words? We can, too, ask things in the contemporary world of art, — does a dot on a wall signify anything more than what it is? How about John Cage's music work '4'33"', a piece entirely silent gathering worldwide fame for its ability to redefine what music is, surely something audible? Poetry is visual, both physically and mentally. I believe that poets must agree to voluntarily, that their poems have the right to be interpreted.

However, when poetry is simply incomprehensible without a multitude of prior knowledge — such as T.S. Eliot's masterpiece 'The Wasteland', we too must savour its lack of simplicity, allowing us to grasp the more indescribable ideas and emotions that are not easily depicted through words. Eliot uses over 40 different works in his work, some of which to Dante's Inferno, Shakespeare plays, German geography, and Hinduism. T.S. Eliot mentioned about his poem, after it being analysed extensively for hours, how it was far beyond what he had intended. Even something so rich as the Wasteland was not filled purposefully with such intentions. However, as analysts, at the bare minimum, we are obliged to find essence in poetry. Eliot's incredibly famous line 'April is the cruelest month' pushes our (Northern Hemisphere) opinions on the joy of the arrival [of spring / September], as if that was unusual. But looking from the perspective of someone who is mentally challenged, and depressed, comfort is found in cold, dark of winter, where their internal & external being are at equilibrium. Surely Eliot didn't intend this, [as it's] rarely evaluated. On [the contrary], we are provided with new perspectives. We can relate to the winter comfort [of the minority] juxtaposed to the 'normal' commoners' 'joy'. But perhaps, more people than we [think] realize Eliot also looks at the Hindu religion — religion being an integral part of humanity. The connection of the earthly thunder, speaking 'DA', reflected in the 3 Hindu words, 'dahtta', 'dayadhvam', and 'damyhata' each for a different figure, humans, gods, & demons. Even through this, Eliot depicts the idea that different creatures will interpret the same thing differently. [We can interpret it] as I do now, and as all English scholars strive to do. But the interpretations are all based heavily on one's own desire to extract their own personal message from the passage. Fry, when treating this poem as a non-simple or child-like poem, believes all poems should be savoured. This is indeed relevant, we must, however, gain new perspectives on similar material, to further broaden our horizons to new possibilities, to enhance our appreciation of poems more, allowing us to savour them to a far greater extent.

Chantelle Xiong, winner of the National (NZ) poetry competition (high school) in 2024, won with her poem entitled 'y but i wish', evaluating the impacts of colonial living on Chinese immigrants, such as herself. This poem is extremely sophisticated in vocabulary, shape, and punctuation, however is [simple] in its readability. 'Child-like' could be seen [as], although it may not be used in any literary context, due to its negatively connotative / more derogatory term. But Xiong's poem indeed is overwhelming at first glance, but is easily understandable when read. Xiong, as the final line of her poem, evaluates how she is treated differently, saying 'but i wish / it didn't have to be'. The consistent use of the lowercase 'i' emphasizes the stereotypical ability of a non-English speaking immigrant at the English language. Also simplistic references to commonly known things like 'the Beatles' band, further emphasizes both the lack of English training she has had, along with the 'white-washing' that occurs in colonial countries, [such as] 'white [person's] food', as her requiring [it] just to help her fit in. The amount of relevance the poem has, not only to the immigrant community, but also to those who interact with them, as a plea for acceptance.

Fry's comment about simple poetry being savoured is true, regardless of the poet's intentions. However, we, as a reader, must attempt to appreciate the simplicity of some poems, before trying to interpret them in our own ways. Even Eliot can be interpreted (ironically), as referring to the inability for cohesion in interpretation. All people have bias, as a result of personal experiences. Our role is to savour all and others' perspectives on our own, to the full extent, to [unlock] the potential, and richness of simple, or not simple, poetry, whatever that means for you.
