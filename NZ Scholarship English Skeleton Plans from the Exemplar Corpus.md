# NZ Scholarship English: Skeleton Plans from the Exemplar Corpus

*Built from 36 exemplar essays (2022–2025). Prepared 20 November 2026.*

**Labels.** OFFICIAL = stated by NZQA or in your brief. OBSERVED = seen in the corpus. Strength words: Corpus-wide (nearly all), Recurrent (several), Individual (one or few), Absence (a plausible feature not used). REC = my recommendation. Guidance silent = NZQA text does not address it.

**Cites.** \[Year-Section-Statement/tier, location\]. Tiers are file labels: O outstanding, T topscholar, S scholarship. ¶ = paragraph. Quotations come from unbracketed text only.

## 2. Cross-section principles

1. **OFFICIAL.** A compares one prose text and one poem. B and C each use one chosen question. B and C show wide knowledge, genre awareness and intertextuality. B and C must not repeat content or quotations.
2. **OBSERVED, Corpus-wide.** All 36 introductions state a claim. In A and B, close reading follows one sequence: quotation, named technique, effect, link to the question.
3. **OBSERVED, Recurrent.** 27 of 36 essays use 3–4 body paragraphs (A 9, B 8, C 10). The paragraphs do different jobs, so "three points" is not a model. Six essays use two long paragraphs.
4. **OBSERVED, graded.** The 2025 comments reward synthesis: "an excellent synthesis of the ideas and the text" (O) and "a woven argument between each text would have been valuable" (S). The questions differ, so treat this as a hint.
5. **Guidance silent.** Paragraph count, roadmaps, first person, essay order and critic references. Full roadmaps are Individual (for example 2023-B/S). Treat these as choices.
6. **REC.** Plan every essay as: answer → test by close reading → limit → qualified judgement. Write the answer in one sentence first.

## 3. Section plans

### Plan A: comparative close reading

**1. What the section demands**

- **OFFICIAL.** Two texts, one prose and one poem. The question asks for a "comparison of the texts with emphasis on aspects of content and crafting" (2026 specification). Corpus wording: "Compare the writers' treatment of nature in Text A and Text B" \[2025-A/O\].
- **REC.** Map how the texts relate in ideas, language, form and effect. Do not list features. Do not write two commentaries and add a comparison at the end.
- **OBSERVED.** Organisation (12 essays): 6 put both texts in every body paragraph; 2 alternate paragraphs by text under a shared theme; 4 write one block per text, then compare. The graded pair: combined = 6 (2025-O); block = 5 (2025-S). The S comment: "a woven argument between each text would have been valuable".
- **OBSERVED, Recurrent.** 4 of 12 move from shared ground, to divergence, to qualified convergence (2023-T, 2022-O, 2024-S, 2024-T). 8 of 12 name a contrast in the introduction.
- **Alternatives.** *Axis-led* (one contrast organises all blocks): use when the texts oppose in attitude \[2024-A/T, 2025-A/T\]. *Stage-led* (ground → split → converge): use when the texts share a subject but end differently \[2023-A/T\]. *Block + synthesis*: fallback only; start comparing in the second block.

**2. Planning decisions (3–5 minutes)**

1. Annotate each text: attitude, two strong techniques, form.
2. Write the answer: "Both texts \_\_\_; A \_\_\_, while B \_\_\_."
3. Name the axis: the one thing that differs.
4. Pick four points: shared ground, language, form, limit.
5. Choose at most two quotations per text per point.
6. Leave out plot retelling, technique lists and outside context.

REC time: 5 minutes plan, 45 writing, 5 checking.

**4. Transitions**

- "Where the texts differ, however, is in their assessment of emotional or logical understanding" \[2023-A/T ¶3\]. Moves from shared ground to the exact point of difference.
- "Both texts suggest that it is not possible to ever cease to persevere" \[2024-A/T ¶4\]. Returns to agreement after a contrast and qualifies the axis.
- "Perhaps, had Sisyphus had companions to shoulder his burden" \[2024-A/T ¶5\]. Changes scale: one image restates the axis.

**5. Flexibility**

- Short on time: put one form sentence inside Block 2, and merge synthesis with conclusion (2022-A/S and 2022-A/T do this).
- No shared ground: drop Block 1 and open with the difference.
- Mechanical risk: every paragraph runs A then B. Swap the order once; \[2024-A/T ¶4\] opens with B.
- Two long body paragraphs (2024-A/O, 2023-A/O) work only if each holds a full compare-and-conclude cycle.

**6. Fillable prompts**

- Intro: "Both texts treat \_\_\_. A \_\_\_, whereas B \_\_\_. The key difference is \_\_\_."
- Block 1: "Both writers \_\_\_, shown by \_\_\_ (A) and \_\_\_ (B)."
- Block 2: "A uses \_\_\_ to \_\_\_. B answers with \_\_\_ to \_\_\_. This shows \_\_\_."
- Block 3: "Prose/poem form makes \_\_\_ because \_\_\_."
- Block 4: "Yet both \_\_\_, which complicates \_\_\_."
- Synthesis: "The texts differ because A wants \_\_\_ and B wants \_\_\_."
- Conclusion: "So the texts show \_\_\_."

### Plan B: responding to literature and language

**1. What the section demands**

- **OFFICIAL.** B offers questions on poetry, non-fiction, film, Shakespeare, drama, novel, short story, TV series and interactive text. Choose one. Show wide knowledge, genre awareness and intertextuality. Do not repeat content or quotations in C (2026 specification). Corpus wording: "In plays, the truth is rarely pure and never simple" \[2025-B/O\]; "Short stories hand you the pieces and what you build is up to you" \[2025-B/S\].
- **REC.** Control scope. Use selected texts to test the statement in depth. Separate what happens from how techniques create meaning. A whole-genre account is not required, and no essay gives one.
- **OBSERVED, texts.** 4 of 12 use one text. 8 of 12 use two to four works. Both work. 2024-B/T limits its claim: "True crime is a microcosm of the genre".
- **OBSERVED, structures.** (a) Criteria drawn from the statement: 4 (2025-O, 2025-T, 2023-S, 2023-O). (b) One work per block under one lens: 3 (2022-S, 2025-S, 2024-O). (c) Chronological arc of one text: 2 (2023-T, 2024-S). (d) Concede → complicate → qualify: 3 (2024-T, 2022-O, 2022-T). No shared paragraph model.
- **Triggers.** Use (a) when the statement names two or more qualities. Use (b) when it claims a genre-wide effect and you know two or three works. Use (c) when it concerns development inside one text. Use (d) when it uses absolute words such as "must" or "barbaric".
- **OBSERVED, graded.** 2025-O (type a, one text) scored 7: "It addressed the statement throughout, never losing sight of it." 2025-S (type b, two texts) scored 5: "stronger synthesis across texts would have strengthened the response".

**2. Planning decisions (3–5 minutes)**

1. Underline loaded words in the statement. Define each in one line.
2. Choose a position (agree, partly, contest). State the tension.
3. Choose texts: one deep text, or two to three that each test a different part. Name only texts you will analyse (2 of 12 do not: 2022-B/S, 2024-B/T).
4. For each text, pick two moments where technique carries meaning. Note technique → effect → link.
5. Choose your limit: the best evidence against your reading.
6. Leave out plot summary and biography. Leave out any text, scene or quotation you need for C.

REC time: 5 minutes plan, 45 writing, 5 checking.

**4. Transitions**

- "Even the inclusion of raw, unadulterated depiction of an event" \[2024-B/T ¶4\]. Tests the strongest defence of the opposite view. Each paragraph removes one defence.
- "However, in "A Woman's Last Word", the" \[2022-B/O ¶4\]. Revises the first reading inside the same text.
- "Unlike the traditional villains we witness" \[2025-B/O ¶4\]. Narrows from a general type to a case that breaks the convention.

**5. Flexibility**

- One text: make blocks scenes or acts, ordered by argument. Use plot order only when the statement concerns development (2023-B/T).
- Two or three works: end each block with a statement link and one cross-text sentence, such as "unlike Flowers for Algernon which still has a plot" \[2025-B/S ¶3\].
- Short on time: merge Blocks 1 and 2, drop Block 4, and fold Block 3 into one sentence in the revised judgement.
- Mechanical risk: concede → complicate in every essay. Use Block 3 only when your texts push back. If the statement has no absolute word, use an extension (change of scale) instead of a limit.

**6. Fillable prompts**

- Intro: "The statement says \_\_\_. Its key term means \_\_\_. I argue \_\_\_, tested through \_\_\_."
- Block 1: "The strongest case for the statement is \_\_\_ in \_\_\_, where \_\_\_ (technique) \_\_\_."
- Block 2: "The technique \_\_\_ makes the reader \_\_\_, which shows \_\_\_."
- Block 3: "This resists the statement because \_\_\_."
- Block 4: "This technique makes sense in the context of \_\_\_ because \_\_\_."
- Revised judgement: "So the better word is \_\_\_, not \_\_\_."
- Synthesis: "These texts show that \_\_\_ in \_\_\_, but only \_\_\_."
- Conclusion: "The statement holds if \_\_\_ and fails if \_\_\_."

### Plan C: exploring issues in literature and language

**1. What the section demands**

- **OFFICIAL.** C offers issue-based questions. Choose one. Show wide knowledge, wide reading, genre awareness and intertextuality. Do not repeat content or quotations from B (2026 specification). Corpus wording: "Ideas travel further than the pages that first held them" \[2025-C/O\]; "Works of literature are interconnected; the more you read, the easier it gets" \[2023-C/S\].
- **REC.** Move from the statement to a claim about literature, then evidence, then cross-text connection, then a qualified judgement. Do not summarise texts in turn. Do not make claims about "all literature".
- **OBSERVED.** All 12 essays cross at least two forms (Corpus-wide). Text count runs from 2 to 9. 6 of 12 use three or four main texts. The 2025-O comment praises "a judicious selection and range of texts".
- **OBSERVED, structures.** Claim-led: 5 (2023-T, 2025-O, 2024-O, 2023-S, 2023-O). Statement-dissection: 3 (2024-T, 2025-T, 2022-O). One text per block: 4 (2024-S, 2022-S, 2022-T, 2025-S). Graded pair: claim-led scored 7 (2025-O); one-text-blocks scored 5 (2025-S), although its comment praises "interlocking rather than opposing ideas".
- **Triggers.** Claim-led: the statement says what literature does. Dissection: the statement has absolute or metaphorical words ("the", "tell", "twin", "utterly different"). One text per block: only if each text answers a different part of the statement and you add a cross-text block.
- **Text roles (REC, with observed examples).** *Anchor*: read deepest \[2024-C/T ¶2\]. *Partner*: another form or era \[2024-C/T ¶3\]. *Complicator*: pushes back \[2025-C/T ¶4\]. *Scale-shifter*: moves to a larger frame \[2025-C/O ¶2\]. *Lens*: a concept that unifies \[2024-C/T ¶5\].

**2. Planning decisions (3–5 minutes)**

1. Restate the statement as your claim about what literature does.
2. Choose a position (agree, partly, contest). State the tension.
3. Choose three or four texts from your C bank, in at least two forms. Use none from B. Give each a role.
4. For each block, write one claim and pick one deepest quotation.
5. Choose your counter-case.
6. Leave out plot summaries, a fifth text without a close quotation, and any B quotation.

REC time: 5 minutes plan, 45 writing, 5 checking.

**4. Transitions**

- "Thus we come to an additional problem with the statement" \[2024-C/T ¶5\]. Tests a further limit with a new text family.
- "While literature seeks to strengthen the walls of our oppressive systems" \[2023-C/O ¶4\]. Turns to a counter-case and reverses direction.
- "This mirrors Mrs Bennet's obsession" \[2025-C/O ¶2\]. Links two texts through one shared issue.

**5. Flexibility**

- Two texts (2025-C/S): give each text one half of the statement, then add a short cross-text block. Without it, blocks become summaries.
- Five or more texts: give extras one sentence inside a block. 2023-C/T uses nine texts. Keep at least two texts under close reading.
- No counter-case: change the scale instead, as 2022-C/S does when it moves to ordinary lives.
- Mechanical risk: "claim → pair → both show" in every block. Make one block a conflict between two texts \[2025-C/T ¶4\].

**6. Fillable prompts**

- Intro: "The statement claims \_\_\_. I argue literature \_\_\_, but only \_\_\_."
- Block 1: "In \_\_\_, the technique \_\_\_ shows \_\_\_."
- Block 2: "\_\_\_ tests this in another form: \_\_\_."
- Block 3: "The idea grows from \_\_\_ to \_\_\_."
- Block 4: "\_\_\_ resists the claim because \_\_\_."
- Block 5: "The concept of \_\_\_ explains why \_\_\_."
- Synthesis: "Together the texts show \_\_\_, within the limit \_\_\_."
- Conclusion: "So the statement is true when \_\_\_ and false when \_\_\_."

### Appendix: three paragraphs as written

**A. \[2024-A/T ¶3\], setting.**

1. Claim: setting shapes connection or isolation.
2. Text A: mixed detail, "very blue" sky beside "thorny branches".
3. Effect: effort is a mixed experience; linked nature implies a group.
4. Pivot: "Conversely, text B employs an urban environment".
5. Text B: "arterial connections", but rails fan out from one centre.
6. Complication: connection hides separation.
7. Second proof: city noise, no people, so isolation.

**B. \[2024-B/T ¶4\], raw evidence.**

1. Claim: even raw depiction fails "stick to the facts".
2. Evidence: the title sequence repeats one crime-scene photo.
3. Objection: repetition "may seem like an emphasis of fact".
4. Rebuttal: it is "incredibly misleading".
5. Theory: Freud on repetition as a substitute for memory.
6. Application: makers repeat because "there are no facts to stick to".
7. Genre claim: the obsession with evidence shows the limit.

**C. \[2024-C/T ¶4\], reading scripture.**

1. Claim: fiction's mask works best against a traditional arbiter of truth.
2. Text 1: Maryam says "we should live lives free from scripture".
3. Effect: the reader starts to doubt.
4. Text 2: Mary says "it was not worth it".
5. Effect: human grief grounds a divine story.
6. Link: utilitarian and Christian frameworks both face questions.
7. Return to the statement: neither text will "tell"; both question.

## 5. Checklists

### Preparation

- **Knowledge.** Pick two or three B genres. Learn one or two texts in each. For C, learn eight to ten texts across at least three forms, each with one idea about literature.
- **Evidence.** B bank: 8–10 short quotations per text, each tagged technique → effect. C bank: 3–4 quotations per text. Keep each quotation under 15 words.
- **Skills.** Write technique → effect → link sentences daily. Define a statement's loaded word in one line. Find one limit for every text.
- **A.** Annotate unseen prose and poem pairs in five minutes. Name the axis. Practise the form paragraph.
- **B and C.** Practise five statements for each. Time each essay at one hour.
- **Keeping B and C apart.** Build two text banks with no shared texts. Keep a ledger: each quotation is B-only or C-only. NZQA bans repeated content and quotations; it is silent on a reused text. REC: avoid reuse. If you must reuse, use a different scene and different quotations.

### In-exam reminders

**A**

1. Annotate for five minutes before writing.
2. Write the answer sentence first.
3. Name the axis in the introduction.
4. Put both texts in each body paragraph.
5. Add one form comparison.
6. Write one "where the contrast bends" sentence.
7. End with why the texts differ.
8. Keep four minutes for the ending.

**B**

1. Underline and define the loaded words.
2. Choose one position and one tension.
3. Name only texts you will analyse.
4. Link every block back to the statement.
5. Tie each quotation to a technique and an effect.
6. Add a limit before the conclusion.
7. Restate the statement in your own precise term.
8. Note your B quotations on a rough page for C.

**C**

1. Check the rough page: no B text, scene or quotation.
2. Restate the statement as your own claim.
3. Give each text a role.
4. Read one text deeply in each block.
5. Link texts through a shared issue, not a list.
6. Include one counter-case or change of scale.
7. Claim only what your texts support.
8. End with a qualified judgement.
